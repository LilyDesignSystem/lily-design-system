Healthcare user interfaces

A guide for teams building clinical software with Lily: clinical portals, results and reporting, prescribing and medicines administration, decision support and hospital dashboards.

Translations of this page are unreviewed machine drafts. The English page is the authoritative version.

What Lily does, and what it does not

Lily gives you display contracts: semantic markup, ARIA, keyboard behaviour and class hooks for the things clinical screens repeat. It does not give you clinical logic, clinical content or a regulatory claim.

Regulation. Software that computes a score, a centile, a dose or a drug interaction is usually a medical device. Lily computes none of these, on purpose. If your product is regulated, you own its usability engineering file (IEC 62366-1, the FDA's human factors guidance, EU MDR). Lily's tests, its self-assessed accessibility conformance report and its screen-reader matrix can be evidence in that file; none of them is a conformance claim, and the matrix has no results yet.

Alerts: interrupt only for what is critical

Alert fatigue is a safety problem: when every alert interrupts, people dismiss them all. AHRQ's guidance is to tier alerts by severity and make only the severe ones interruptive.

  • Information (indicator="info"): inline, beside the task, a decision-support card in the page.
  • Warning (indicator="warning"): visible without blocking, the same card, styled to stand out; no modal.
  • Critical (indicator="critical"): put the card inside an alert dialog, and offer overrideReasons so dismissing it asks for a reason.
  • A decision-support card is never a live region by itself. Announce a new critical card by moving focus into the dialog, not by aria-live.
  • Remove a card when the event it answered happens again, and record each outcome (accepted, or overridden with its reason).

Wrong-patient errors: identify, do not restrict

  • Show two identifiers wherever a patient's data appears: the name with the date of birth, and the national identifier with its view component (The Joint Commission, Quick Safety 45).
  • Use the patient banner on every screen that acts on one patient's record, and keep it visible.
  • Before an action on a record other than the one in the banner, confirm it with the name, date of birth and identifier.
  • Do not limit how many records a clinician may open: a randomised trial of 3,356 clinicians (JAMA, 2019) found that it did not reduce wrong-patient orders, and people work around it.

The patient banner

The composition is documented with medical-banner, with a working page at /patient-banner in the SvelteKit example app:

  • two identifiers, and the age beside the date of birth;
  • allergies through an allergy list, so "no known allergies", "not recorded" and "unavailable" never look alike;
  • a missing value is words ("Not known"), never a question mark, a dash or an empty cell;
  • a deceased patient: data-deceased for the theme, and a visible line of text, never colour alone;
  • no field truncated, clipped or hidden at any width; it wraps.

Results

  • One display for a result, whatever system it came from: the observation view.
  • The flag is words from your translation (flagLabel), with the FHIR interpretation code as data-interpretation for the theme.
  • Separate new results from earlier ones, and show a trend (a sparkline, or a line chart with its data table): clinicians look for a significant change, not only the range.
  • A result that was never recorded and one that is unavailable are different states, with different words.

Medicines

  • Generic names in lower case, except tall man letters from the FDA and ISMP lists or your country's (Australia and Canada publish their own). Mark them in nameSegments; Lily never guesses them.
  • Write the strength with formatQuantity: no trailing zero, a leading zero, a no-break space before the unit.
  • Keep a result's comparator: formatQuantity(5, "mmol/L", locale, "<") writes "<5 mmol/L". FHIR's Quantity.comparator is written <, ≤, ≥ or >; dropping it turns "less than 5" into "5".
  • Write mL, never cc, and avoid every abbreviation on ISMP's error-prone list.
  • Never truncate a medicine's name or dose.

Dates and times

  • Write the month as text so a date cannot be read two ways (Intl.DateTimeFormat with month: "short").
  • Choose the clock on purpose: pass hourCycle explicitly. The NHS asks for the 24-hour clock; that is a UK rule, not a worldwide one, so Lily never picks it for you.
  • Ages follow the Common User Interface bands through ageParts(); a service with another convention passes its own words.

Dashboards

  • A ward board or command-centre screen updates often. Never announce each change: keep one polite summary region ("3 beds free, 2 discharges due") and let people read the board.
  • Every state on a board (bed clean, occupied, blocked) is text or a data-* state with words, never colour alone.

Map an entered-in-error record to its entered-in-error status, with words, rather than dropping it silently: whether to show it at all is your service's decision.

Coded values

  • Show Coding.display; if there is none, CodeableConcept.text; if neither, system|code. Never an empty cell.
  • Offer a control that reveals the code system and code beside each value, such as a toggle button that says "Show codes". Hidden codes stay out of the reading order until shown.
  • Mark clinical text with the document's language (lang), which may differ from the interface's. Translate the interface; never translate the clinical content.
  • Lily ships no terminology: no SNOMED CT, LOINC, ATC or UCUM lookups. Write what the document says. The SvelteKit example's /patient-summary page shows the pattern.

What Lily will not do

  • Compute an early warning score, a growth centile, a dose or a reference range.
  • Ship a drug database, a tall man list, or SNOMED CT, ICD, LOINC or UCUM codes.
  • Reproduce a licensed instrument (Wong-Baker FACES) or another body's design (the NEWS2 chart).
  • Limit the number of records open at once.
